# History, maintenance, and encoding

Anni McHenry / Fieldlight Institute · November 10, 2026  
Research extension from October 21–November 9 source notes. Designs retained; experiments below have not yet been run.

A record can survive without changing what a system does. A system can also retain the effects of an encounter without keeping an account that a participant can read. Active Substrates must distinguish these cases. Its next question is what retained history makes possible, what it costs to maintain that possibility, and who can recognize or revise it.

## 1. History that changes the next response

The November 2 inquiry begins with a tree that survives fire: where does the event persist, and how does that persistence affect a later disturbance? The object of study is the difference between a readable record of an event and a change in the system's response because the event occurred. The November 9 return to an earlier tree-ring passage adds a separate question about recognition: the same surviving representation can acquire a new function when the reader's conceptual tools change.

Use a state-space model as a starting specification:

\[
x_{t+1}=F(x_t,u_t,m_t;\theta),\qquad
m_{t+1}=\lambda m_t+G(x_t,u_t).
\]

Here, \(u_t\) is an input or disturbance, \(x_t\) the modeled current condition, and \(m_t\) retained effects not already contained in \(x_t\). The memory term must not double-count a sufficiently specified current state. Its value is tested by predictive improvement, perturbation, and recovery under new inputs.

**First computational comparison:** expose matched systems to different histories, then present the same held-out disturbance. Compare retained history, reset history, and an equally expressive present-state model. Charge storage and update costs. Measure response trajectory, recovery time, and future feasible work, including cases in which history increases vulnerability. Estimate persistence on development sequences; test it on separate sequences.

**Ecological branch:** document specimen identity, collection permission, position on the tree, species-identification confidence, and independently supported disturbance dates before using a slice as a time series. A scar is an observation; a cause-specific history and a subsequent response require additional evidence. Cross-species work must account for different growth, injury and preservation processes. No specimen measurement or biological result is claimed in this release.

**Transfer test:** move a mathematical structure between domains only after defining the variables and measurement process in each. A successful forest fit is not a human prediction. A prospective physiological comparison must show that the shared structure improves held-out prediction over domain-specific baselines and requires its own human-study arrangements.

## 2. A medium that changes its own pathways

The November 2 Pythia's Oasis note identifies coupled substrate dynamics and indirect observation as distinct additions. It proposes examining transport whose pathways change through the activity they support. The geophysical source identified in that note is [the 2023 paper retained at PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9876559/); its detailed measurements and mechanism claims remain a source-review task before a quantitative geophysical model is specified.

The computational experiment can proceed without borrowing unverified measurements. Compare a fixed network, an activity-responsive network, and a network receiving replayed changes from another run. Hold input sequences, operation budgets and initial connectivity fixed. Distinguish immediate physical responsiveness, durable history dependence, and improvement through learning. Measure whether allowing pathway state to vary improves predictions and task outcomes after disturbances.

For indirect observation, define \(y_t=h(x_t,m_t;\theta)+\epsilon_t\). Test which internal histories remain distinguishable from the permitted observations. Deliberately include different histories that generate the same observed output. Recovering a signal does not necessarily recover the history that produced it; the experiment must report ambiguity as well as reconstruction accuracy.

## 3. Maintaining the capacity to participate

The October 30 note makes maintenance an object of intelligence research: sustaining the relationships and material conditions under which further work remains possible. It asks whether capacities classified as care, cultivation, or maintenance have been excluded from the account of what intelligence does.

Extend the simulation budget to include upkeep, repair, memory refresh, and replacement. Compare policies maximizing immediate completions with policies preserving future feasible service under matched total resources. Track who pays maintenance costs, which capabilities disappear, and how much work remains possible when a participant refuses or leaves. Maintenance is not an unexplained free resource, nor is aggregate survival sufficient if its cost is concentrated on participants without authority to revise their involvement.

The associated historical inquiry concerns women's production and transmission of knowledge, Indigenous epistemologies, and the sources of behavioral information inherited by artificial systems. The originating proposition, **intelligence is inherently feminine**, remains a research thesis whose evolutionary, historical, and information-theoretic formulations require different evidence. None is replaced by a scheduling result. Gregory Cajete's *Native Science: Natural Laws of Interdependence* is a designated reading direction; particular communities' practices and Cajete's arguments require direct study rather than treatment as a generic analogy. Commercial behavior, advertising, and model-training records must be traced as distinct sources.

## 4. Encoding and the conditions of recognition

The October 21 Vault Flower note makes encoding explicit between receiving information and preserving it. Its useful addition is that relationships and decoding conventions may carry information that individual record contents do not. It separates decoding, recognition, and authority to act.

Test the same bounded source set under three conditions: records alone, records with typed relationships, and records with relationships plus a versioned decoding schema. Match available information and resource budgets; document when a condition necessarily contains additional information. Introduce participant replacement and schema changes. Measure recoverable facts, relational queries, source-attribution accuracy, unauthorized actions, and successor performance. Include a control with the same information serialized in a single representation so a topology advantage is not merely an information advantage.

A second comparison changes the interpreter while retaining the record. Preserve competing interpretations with the context and method that produced them. Score a task with an independently defined answer where one exists; do not manufacture a single correct portrait of a person. Cross-context recognition and productive difference can then be investigated without making interpretive uniformity the goal.
